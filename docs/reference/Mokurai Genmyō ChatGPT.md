The present temple is unusual physically as well: *Wedge* reports that the "temple" occupies a former abandoned inn, a three-story wooden building rather than a conventional monumental Buddhist temple. ([Wedge ONLINE(ウェッジ・オンライン)](https://wedge.ismedia.jp/articles/-/40342?layout=b))

That actually seems very appropriate to Mokurai's philosophy.

He doesn't seem particularly interested in building an impressive institution.

He is interested in **using an institution to do something**.

# 18. His Rinzai lineage matters — but he deliberately moved beyond sectarian identity

I'd characterize his religious development this way:

### Phase 1 — Rinzai discipline

**松尾太年 → 長屋真乗**

Traditional Zen training.

### Phase 2 — asceticism

**今東光**

A Tendai priest, public intellectual and political figure.

### Phase 3 — pilgrimage

**乞食雲水**

Seven-and-a-half years of walking and living outside institutional structures.

### Phase 4 — street Zen

**下座行**

Testing Buddhism against ordinary society.

### Phase 5 — social Buddhism

**地域再生**

Trying to repair communities rather than merely teaching individuals.

### Phase 6 — ecological Buddhism

**いのちの森**

Repairing the relationship between people, death, forests and communities.

That trajectory makes much more sense than simply saying:

> "He was a Rinzai monk who founded his own sect."

# 19. One sentence in his biography now strikes me differently

He writes:

> **人をのみ渡し渡して　おのが身は
> 岸に上がらぬ　渡し守かな**

Something like:

> **I ferry people across, one after another,
> yet I myself never reach the shore —
> like the ferryman.**

This is an extraordinarily good key to understanding Mokurai.

He seems to see the monk's role not as reaching some private spiritual destination and then settling comfortably in a temple.

The monk remains **in the river**.

That explains:

- walking,
- fasting,
- sleeping under bridges,
- street meditation,
- rural reconstruction,
- forest work,
- cemetery reform,
- and his continued political/social engagement.

## 20. And there is a striking connection to Mitsue

This is where I think the research becomes particularly interesting for you.

Mokurai and your **Mitsue 75/25-year concept** are obviously not the same project.

But they share a surprisingly deep structural idea:

| Mokurai                           | Mitsue concept                      |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| damaged mountain                  | damaged monoculture forest          |
| abandoned rural community         | declining rural community           |
| forest treated as a resource      | forest treated as a legacy          |
| use forest to sustain community   | use biomass to sustain community    |
| forest restoration                | broadleaf replanting                |
| death returns to forest           | old sugi returns as energy          |
| forest generates economic support | biomass generates energy            |
| energy/economics serves life      | computation/AI serves community     |
| future generations                | 25-year future                      |
| spiritual responsibility          | spiritual responsibility            |
| **いのちの森**                    | **forest becoming light/knowledge** |

The crucial difference is that **Mokurai's mechanism is death → forest**, while your Mitsue mechanism is closer to **old forest → energy → knowledge → future forest**.

But both are trying to solve the same deeper problem:

> **How can something inherited from the past be transformed into something that continues to serve life in the future?**

And that is probably why his story feels unusually relevant to your Mitsue work.
