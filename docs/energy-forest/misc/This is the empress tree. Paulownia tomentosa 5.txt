After the trees are harvested, the trunks are cut into sections of about one meter and then sorted by different diameter classes. The bundles sorted by diameter are then naturally pre-dried here on the log yard for about six weeks before they go into production. In a parallel circular saw, the log sections are now sawn on four sides so that they can be stacked on top of each other and then transported to the drying chamber in the next step. The three log sections in front of me come from a Kiri tree that grew here in Thüns Forest on the Lower Rhine for three and a half years. The age is also clearly visible in the growth rings. From these different diameter classes, we then produce octagonal profiles with appropriately adjusted dimensions in the next step, because this way we can optimize the utilization efficiency of the solid wood. We achieve solid wood utilization rates of sometimes over seventy percent. The raw profiles, technically dried to a moisture content of about fifteen percent, are now passed through an industrial planer, which produces octagonal profiles, the so-called octagon profiles, in a single operation. Here on the drilling line, the planed octagonal profiles are drilled from two axes and then go directly to assembly from here. The finished drilled and chamfered octagonal profiles are now connected in assembly with these pine wood system dowels. They are very sharply milled dowels. Pine wood is harder than the relatively light Kiri wood with a weight of only two hundred fifty kilograms. This allows us to completely avoid using glue and other metallic connections, as the harder pine wood creates a permanent and very structurally strong bond when driven into the holes.
