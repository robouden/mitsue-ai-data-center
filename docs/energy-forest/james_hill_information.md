## Research focus

His project combines several disciplines:

- Palaeoecology
- Archaeobotany
- Environmental archaeology
- Landscape history
- Japanese sacred forests (鎮守の森 / chinju no mori)

The aim is to reconstruct how Japanese sacred groves have changed over hundreds or thousands of years by analysing evidence such as:

- fossil pollen
- charcoal
- phytoliths (microscopic silica bodies from plants)
- sediments
- historical vegetation records

This helps distinguish:

- natural forest development,
- human management,
- shrine maintenance,
- agricultural impacts,
- biodiversity conservation.

The work has applications in:

- cultural heritage preservation,
- ecology,
- restoration of satoyama landscapes,
- climate and environmental history.

## Why this research matters

Japanese shrine forests are often described as "ancient forests," but many have actually been shaped by centuries of human management.

Questions this research can answer include:

- Which tree species originally grew there?
- When were cedar or cypress plantations introduced?
- How did medieval or Edo-period land use affect forests?
- Which forests truly preserve ancient biodiversity?

These findings can inform both historical scholarship and modern conservation practices. ([Japan Space Policy Secretariat](https://www.jsps.go.jp/file/storage/saiyo_2026/2026_1-e.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))
