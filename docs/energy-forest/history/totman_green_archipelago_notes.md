# Notes: *The Green Archipelago* (Conrad Totman, 1989) — for CHP/reforestation planning

Source: `the-green-archipelago-forestry-in-pre-industrial-japan_compress.pdf` (this folder). Read: Ch.4 (The Negative Regimen), Ch.5 (Silviculture), Ch.6 (Plantation Forestry: Economic Aspects), Ch.7 (Land-Use Patterns and Afforestation), Conclusion. Chapters 1–3 (ancient/medieval predation) not covered in detail — general historical background only.

Totman's core argument: Tokugawa Japan (1600–1868) converted from exploitation forestry to regenerative plantation forestry not from environmental ethics but because (a) land tenure was redefined so specific people had durable, sole stakes in specific parcels, and (b) sustained urban demand (Edo/Osaka/Kyoto fires and construction) made a 50-year timber investment pay off. That pairing — tenure clarity + a reliable long-horizon buyer — is the same structural problem this project is solving for Sugano/Mitsue, with the AI data center's CHP fuel demand playing the role Edo's timber market played historically.

## 1. Sugi/hinoki plantation silviculture technique (Ch.5)

- **Seedbed cycle**: sow soaked seed in fall-prepared beds → thin/transplant at 1–3 yrs → out-plant at 3–4 yrs (stems 1–3 ft). Typical density ~3–4 seedlings per tsubo (~36 sq ft), i.e. roughly one seedling per 9–12 sq ft.
- **Slip (cutting) propagation**: sugi roots easily from cuttings — cheaper than seedbed culture, historically spread Kyushu → northward. Relevant if nursery cost is a bottleneck for reforesting Sugano.
- **"Yoshino method"** (cited by Okino Takao, Kurobane han, 1849): plant sugi at 3-ft intervals → remove every other stem at 2–3" butt diameter → thin again 5 yrs later, selling the poles → final spacing ~12 ft, producing high-grade board stock. This is a concrete, citable historical spacing/thinning schedule.
- **Mortality without aftercare is severe**: recorded survival rates ranged 4–87%, commonly ~50% even with care. Weeding 2–3×/yr for the first 5 years, replacement planting for ~4 years, first limbing at 12–15 yrs, repeated at 5-yr intervals — a plantation is a 50-year commitment, and **aftercare, not initial planting, is the dominant lifetime cost** (see §2).
- **Coppice management** (broadleaf regrowth from cut stumps/roots) was the standard method for fuelwood/charcoal, not conifer timber — it regenerates faster than seed-grown conifer stands and requires far less capital or skilled labor. Directly relevant to CHP fuel-supply planning: a coppice-based fuel woodlot is a fundamentally cheaper, faster-cycling asset than a sugi/hinoki timber plantation, and historically the two were run as separate land-use categories on the same mountain.

## 2. Plantation economics (Ch.6)

- **Initial planting cost**: ~5–15 momme per 100 conifers set out (silver momme, Edo-period currency — not directly convertible to yen, cited for relative-cost structure only).
- **Aftercare cost dominates**: over the decades, aftercare could total up to ~80% of a plantation's total pre-harvest cost. Any modern cost model for the Sugano plantation should weight ongoing labor (weeding, thinning, limbing, patrol) far more heavily than planting.
- **What made plantation forestry pay historically** (three conditions, all needed together):
  1. Proximity to transport/market — stands sited streamside or along known routes to cities beat exploitation loggers on shipping cost alone.
  2. Brand-name/quality premium — Yoshino sugi commanded top price for sake-barrel staves, Obi sugi for ship planking, because buyers trusted the species/region pairing for a specific end use.
  3. Labor cost partially absorbed as "communal"/off-season corvee work, not fully monetized — this kept plantation lumber price-competitive with wild-harvested timber.
- Historical example (Ki river): lumber output rose from ~4,000 m³/yr (late 1760s) to ~21,000 m³/yr (late 1780s) as plantations matured and a market existed to absorb them — evidence that yield scales up fast once the economics work, not slowly.
- Plantation woodland was **not taxed as agriculture** under Tokugawa rule, increasing its relative return over cropland — a possible precedent to cite if arguing for favorable tax/subsidy treatment of managed forest vs. neglected forest with the village or prefecture.
- **The buyer is the load-bearing variable.** Without a reliable long-term buyer, plantation forestry didn't happen even where land and know-how existed (most of Japan's woodland was never converted to plantation — only areas near markets were). This is the direct historical parallel to the CHP/AI-DC argument: the data center's steady fuel demand is what Edo's construction/fire-rebuild market was for Yoshino and Ome growers.

## 3. Land-tenure mechanisms that unlocked long-term investment (Ch.7)

Three institutional shifts converted "everyone's, no one's" common woodland — where no one had reason to invest 50 years of care — into land worth managing. All three moved forestry from *multiple-use* (everyone takes a bit of everything) to *single-use with a designated primary use* (one party has priority, others get residual/secondary rights):

- **Wariyama** ("mountain dividing"): communal woodland split into household-assigned parcels, ending free-rider degradation of commons. Documented triggers were usually failed communal management (overcutting, disputes) forcing a village to formally divide land by household. Direct analogy: clarifying exactly which Sugano plots the forest cooperative / Mitsue Kanko / more trees / 丹羽製材 arrangement controls long-term, rather than leaving it as ambiguous shared access.
- **Nenkiyama**: an advance sale of the *standing crop*, not the land. A villager plants and tends the trees; a timber merchant buys the future stand and pays up front; the villager keeps usufruct of the undergrowth (fertilizer/fodder material) until harvest, when the merchant pays a small final fee and takes the timber. This is functionally a forward-supply contract that doesn't require land ownership or land transfer — a plausible template for how a CHP operator or 丹羽製材 could pre-purchase future wood/chip supply from Sugano landholders while they retain and work the land.
- **Buwakebayashi**: yield-sharing between landholder and financier/planter — a proto profit-share joint venture, used especially where government and private capital combined to fund afforestation.

Historical afforestation scaled through **all three mechanisms plus commercial nurseries** (e.g., Kamikata/Ikeda seedling nurseries near Osaka shipped seedlings nationwide by the hundreds of thousands per month by the late 18th century) — i.e., the eventual "boom" phase depended on cheap, reliable seedling supply chains as much as on land-tenure reform.

## 4. Governance patterns (Ch.4) — cautions and framing

- **Tomeyama** (closed/protected forest areas) and **tomeki** (reserved-species rules, e.g. no felling of sugi/hinoki above a certain girth) were the core tools of Tokugawa forest regulation, but Totman's case studies show they **only worked well when they happened to coincide with what villagers already wanted**. Example: on Mount Amagi (Izu), kashi oak was the one reserved species that actually flourished under protection — because it was also useful to villagers as fuel/fertilizer/mast, so they didn't destroy the seedlings even while cutting everything else. Where reserved species had no local use value, protection largely failed. **Caution for species selection**: any reforestation species list (native broadleaf for wildlife forage, per [[project_reforestation_principle]]) needs buy-in from whoever actually works the land (丹羽製材, forest crews), not just ecological/NGO criteria, or enforcement will quietly fail the way tomeki often did.
- **Protection forestry (erosion/flood control) preceded production forestry** as a government priority historically — rulers acted on watershed/flood risk before they acted on timber scarcity. Useful framing if pitching reforestation to the village council: lead with disaster-prevention/erosion-control benefit, treat timber/biomass yield as the secondary payoff, mirroring the historical sequence.
- Forest patrols (yamamori) were a standing, paid or stipended local job — inspecting boundaries, replacement planting, theft/fire — with pay sometimes set as a percentage of sale income (Yoshino: ~5% of sale income to the yamamori). A modern equivalent role (paid forest-monitoring position tied to CHP fuel-supply integrity) has direct historical precedent.
