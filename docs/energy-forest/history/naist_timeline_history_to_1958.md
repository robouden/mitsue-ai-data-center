# NAIST Talk — History Segment: Japanese Forestry, Start → 1958 (~10 min)

Structure of talk: **History → Now → Future → Q&A**. This is the History block, ending at 1958 (Rob's birth year) as the hinge into "Now."

## Timeline

**1603–1867 — Tokugawa era, tenure foundations**
Land held as feudal-lord or communal (commons) tenure; individual land ownership banned in principle. Kiso (feudal) forests tightly guarded, theft severely punished. (Iwamoto)

**1665 — Kiso, 1st reform**
Cut production halved via seedling protection, patrols, selective cutting — reversed within years for revenue. (Iwamoto)

**Late 1600s — Wave 1 of plantation forestry**
Edo and Osaka boom under the Shogunate; sustained fire/construction demand makes 50-year timber investment pay off for the first time. Local custom starts recognizing private ownership of individually-planted trees even on communal land (Yoshino, Owase) — the key institutional unlock. (Iwamoto, Totman)

**1724 — Kiso, 2nd reform**
Cut production down >60%, this time held for 30 years — forest recovers. (Iwamoto)

**1750s — Yamaguni guild adapts**
Rural timber guild (kabu nakama) creates a new license class (ikada han kabu) to admit plantation-grower newcomers without losing collective control — rural producers start out-organizing urban merchants. (Totman)

**1779 — Kiso**
First forest inventory/planning — early sustained-yield thinking. (Iwamoto)

**1801 — Edo**
Population reaches ~1,000,000 (bigger than London or Paris) — isolationist policy blocks timber imports, so demand pressure falls entirely on domestic plantation forestry. (Iwamoto)

**1849 — "Yoshino method" codified**
Okino Takao (Kurobane han) documents plant-at-3ft → thin at 2–3" butt → rethin at 5yr → final 12ft spacing — a citable Edo-era silviculture schedule still recognizable today. (Totman)

**1868 — Meiji Restoration**
Formal land ownership introduced; national forests carved from communal + former feudal land.

**1871–76 — Kan/Min division of woodland**
The Meiji state tries to force all Japanese woodland into government (kan'yuchi) vs. private (min'yuchi) — six major policy reversals in five years. Villagers need gen'ya (mixed scrub, fodder/fuel); the state wants sanrin (dense conifer). Koyuchi (shared-use) emerges as a third, pragmatic category. (Totman)

**1889 — Kan/Min outcome**
Government forest reaches 53% of Japan's woodland (up from 31% in 1880) — a messy, majority-not-total split, residual use-rights linger on both sides.

**1889–90 — Goryōrin founded**
Meiji state carves out ~1.6M ha as the Imperial Household's own income-producing forestry estate. (Totman)

**1892 — Goryōrin turns profitable**
After 1–2 loss-making years — the start of a 58-year run as a real sustained-yield P&L.

**1897 — First Forest Law**yes

Regulation-focused.

**1899–1915 — National forest survey**
16-year project classifies all forest as "necessary"/"unnecessary," sells the latter to fund management of the rest. (Iwamoto)

**Early 1900s — Wave 2 of plantation forestry**
Industrial growth plus Sino-Japanese (1894–95) and Russo-Japanese (1904–05) war reconstruction demand drives the second national planting boom. (Iwamoto)

**1907 — Second Forest Law**
Shifts emphasis to utilization as industrial growth demands efficient production.

**1916 — Goryōrin**
Provides 19% of the Imperial Household's total income.

**1920s–30s — Goryōrin**
Annual net profit climbs from ~234,000 yen (late 1890s) toward ~6,000,000 yen.

**1929–1949 — The cutover-land crisis begins**
Annual cut area exceeds planted area nationally *every single year* for two decades; the gap peaks mid-1940s. (Matsushita)

**1936–45 — Goryōrin at its peak**
40% net profit margin on gross income (vs. 23% for the much larger National Forest) — best-documented proof point that managed Japanese forestry was, for decades, a real bankable asset, not a subsidized cost center.

**1940 — Goryōrin**
31% of Imperial Household income.

**1942–44 — Wartime override**
"Hundred Year Plan" formally suspended; "special harvesting" authorized. Harvest jumps 8.6M koku (1942) → 10.97M koku (1944), achieved by cutting reforestation/aftercare spending — the clearest documented case of short-term demand breaking a sustained-yield plan.

**1946 — Goryōrin**
Cumulative asset value reaches 2.6 billion yen — 70% of the Imperial Household's total wealth.

**1947 — Goryōrin dissolved**
All Imperial property nationalized after the war; 58-year enterprise ends.

**1948 — Cutover land**
Totals 1.49M ha (~6% of Japan's forest area), concentrated near villages. Contemporary GHQ/Forestry Agency studies (wrongly, in hindsight) forecast domestic wood exhaustion within 15–33 years — the political urgency behind everything that follows. (Matsushita)

**1949–1953 — 5-Year Forestation Plan**
First full-scale postwar plan targeting the 1.5M ha of cutover land; budget shortfalls hold actual execution to 60–70%.

**1950 — The demand floor drops out**
Fossil fuels begin replacing firewood/charcoal, chemical fertilizer replaces green fertilizer, machinery replaces draft animals — the whole traditional-use structure that made ordinary villagers' woodland (gen'ya) valuable starts to evaporate within a couple of decades. (Totman) — this is the seed of the *modern* problem, arriving right as postwar replanting ramps up.

**1951 — Forest Act amended**
Planting made *compulsory* for the first time (Article 14) — prefectural governors can legally obligate owners to plant. Same year: log import tariffs cut to near-zero, quietly setting up the self-sufficiency collapse decades later. (Matsushita)

**1952–1961 — 10-Year Forestation Plan**
Goal: industrial-roundwood self-sufficiency via broadleaf→conifer conversion (0.84M ha planned).

**1954 — Peak planting year**
Highest-ever annual planted area recorded under the 10-Year Plan.

**1955 — Wave 3 begins: the largest boom**
Postwar/Korean War economic recovery drives the biggest of Japan's three plantation-forestry booms, running 1955–1970. Artificial planting peaks this year and stays high to 1970. (Iwamoto)

**1955–1960 — 6-Year Forestation Plan**
Shifts from wartime-reconstruction *quantity* to *quality* — nursery stock, seed-tree systems, "right tree, right site."

**1956 — Forest Development Corporation founded**
National vehicle (plus prefectural corporations from 1959) to plant the least economically attractive land using long-term debt against a 40–50-year-out harvest payoff — the financing model that, decades later, leaves all 47 prefectural forestry corporations in debt distress once stumpage prices collapse after 1980. Not yet visible in 1958, but the seed is planted this year.

## 1958 — Rob's birth year: where the hinge sits

By 1958, Japan is deep inside the largest of its three forestation booms (1955–1970), planting compulsory since 1951, self-sufficiency still near ~95% and about to begin its long decline, and the debt-financed corporate planting model about to be formalized (the Profit-Sharing Forestation Act follows this same year). The sugi and hinoki now standing mature in Mitsue's mountains were planted in exactly this window — for a construction-timber and firewood/charcoal economy that fossil fuels had already begun quietly dismantling since 1950, and that cheap imports would finish off within another generation. **The trees are older than the demand that justified planting them.** That's the hinge into "Now."

5. 1929–1949 crisis: cut outpaces planting for 20 straight years; 1.49M ha cutover by 1948.
6. 1949–1957 postwar plans: 5-year → 10-year → 6-year, compulsory planting from 1951.
7. 1950 fossil-fuel pivot + 1955 peak boom, side by side — two curves crossing.
8. 1958 hinge slide → hands off into "Now."
