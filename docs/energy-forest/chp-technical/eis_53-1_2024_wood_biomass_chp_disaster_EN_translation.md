# Case Validation of Woody Biomass CHP for Use in Disasters

*(English translation of: Katoh, Yamazaki, Takaguchi, Miyazaki. "災害時に活用可能な木質バイオマスCHPのケース検証" [報告], 環境情報科学 53-1, 2024, pp.87–94. Translated from Japanese; original is a "報告" (report), not a peer-reviewed full paper.)*

**Authors:** Sayaka Katoh*, Keita Yamazaki**, Hiroto Takaguchi***, Kenichi Miyazaki****
*Waseda University; **Chuden CTI (中国電力CTI), now Shimane Prefectural University; ***Waseda University; ****Chuden CTI

## Abstract
This study focuses on small-scale wood-biomass combined heat and power (CHP) systems that contribute to building a regionally circulating and ecological sphere by using local wood as fuel in normal times, and proposes case-specific system designs so the same CHP can serve as an independent electricity/heat source in disasters. First, for an existing pellet-fired biomass CHP, environmental performance (CO₂ reduction) and economic performance (regional internal multiplier, LM3/LM4) are evaluated in normal operation, and the matching between electricity/heat supply and the Quality-of-Life (QOL) requirements of Disaster Level 2 at an evacuation shelter is examined during a disaster, extracting issues. Next, a new chip-fired biomass CHP is evaluated for environmental/economic performance in normal times, and the matching of electricity/heat supply and demand during a disaster is verified, in both normal and disaster conditions.

**Keywords:** Biomass CHP, Regional Circular and Ecological Sphere, LM3, CO₂ reduction, QOL

## Introduction
Japan's 2018 Fifth Basic Environment Plan proposed the concept of a "Regional Circular and Ecological Sphere" — maximizing the use of each region's resources while complementing and supporting other regions according to local characteristics, to achieve both self-reliance and mutual support. Woody biomass energy is well suited to this: it is carbon-neutral and also gives economic value to previously discarded thinnings, contributing to the use of local forest resources and to regional economic revitalization (Yamazaki, 2021).

Meanwhile, Japan continues to experience large-scale natural disasters. Since the 2021 Atami debris-flow disaster, decentralized, small-scale power sources at evacuation shelters have been reported as effective (Chiba Prefecture Chosei Town's "Machizukuri Council Report," 2021). Biomass CHP installed in such buildings could stably supply both power and heat during disasters, contributing to residents' quality of life. Prior research on resilience evaluation methods for evacuation shelters (Takano, 2013) defines QOL as "the extent to which opportunities for the activities residents value, given the circumstances they find themselves in during a disaster, can be secured" and organizes QOL evaluation into four levels: Level 1 (life protection), Level 2 (health/hygiene protection), Level 3 (social-life protection), Level 4 (cultural-life protection). In Hiratacho, Hokkaido, biomass CHP has been used to secure lighting power for evacuation shelters. Going forward, system designs that satisfy QOL requirements during disasters are needed for biomass CHP more broadly.

The authors take the view that, when power and water/sewage supply are cut in a disaster, an evacuation shelter should be able to secure QOL Level 2 (health/hygiene protection), and that the electricity and heat needed for the equipment meeting this QOL level should be supplied by biomass CHP.

This paper proposes a "disaster response system" that satisfies both regional circular-economy value in normal times and Level 2 QOL during disasters, by examining individual-case system designs for a biomass CHP.

First, for an existing biomass CHP, the effectiveness of CO₂ reduction from forest-resource utilization is evaluated, along with regional internal economic multipliers. Next, the electricity/heat demand for QOL Level 2 requirements at evacuation shelters during disasters, and the matching of that demand with electricity/heat supplied from biomass CHP, are evaluated, and issues extracted. Finally, the environmental/economic performance in normal operation, and the matching of electricity/heat supply and demand during a disaster, are examined for a newly proposed chip-fired biomass CHP, and the future prospects for utilizing both existing and new biomass CHP under normal and disaster conditions are considered.

## 1. Research Method
### 1.1 Normal-time evaluation method
#### 1.1.1 Environmental evaluation
First, an energy-flow diagram is created for the flow of energy arising from forest resources and from the biomass CHP. Values in the flow diagram are calculated from interview results or from prior studies (Yamazaki, 2021). Next, the CO₂ reduction from the biomass CHP is calculated. This study targets the reduction attributable to power generated by biomass CHP and to utilized exhaust heat, compared with the equivalent amount derived from fossil fuel, and the emissions reduction at the fuel (pellet) production plant. The CO₂ reduction obtained is then divided by the weight of raw logs required to produce the biomass CHP fuel, giving the CO₂ reduction per tonne of raw log, which is used for comparison.

#### 1.1.2 Economic evaluation
To evaluate the impact of biomass CHP on the regional economy, this study calculates and compares the regional internal multipliers LM3 and LM4 (Yamazaki, 2021) against values in existing literature.

### 1.2 Structure of the disaster response system
This study assumes a six-day period during which power and water/sewage supply are cut by a natural disaster. Taking the view that among the Level 2 QOL requirements (Takano, 2013) at an evacuation shelter during a disaster, "securing health/hygiene protection" should be prioritized, this study addresses the equipment that requires energy to satisfy this requirement. The devices needed to satisfy each QOL requirement — the "QOL equipment" — are set as shown in **Table 1**. The equipment currently owned and its specifications/quantities by the target buildings form the basis for calculating the power demand.

**Table 1: QOL equipment settings**

|---|---|
| a. Bathing opportunity | Groundwater pump, water-supply pump, purification tank |
| b. Securing food | Rice cooker, refrigerator, kettle |
| c. Toilet environment | Groundwater pump, water-supply pump, purification tank |
| d. Clothing hygiene | Washing machine |
| e. Thermal (heating/cooling) environment | Air conditioner |

Next, a "disaster response system" is built so that the biomass CHP delivers maximum effectiveness in meeting QOL requirements during a disaster. Figure 1 shows the required elements of the disaster response system in normal times and during a disaster, as follows:
 i. Energy supply to the accepting facility for evacuees (a system enabling off-grid operation during a disaster)

 iv. Groundwater use raised by pump, with wastewater treated via a purification tank

### 1.3 Disaster response system verification method
The verification method for the disaster response system is as follows.
**(1) Calculating shelter capacity: A**
Among the target facility's spaces, those usable as shelter space are set, and their floor area after that use is divided by 3.5 m² per evacuee (Sophia Handbook, 2018) to calculate shelter capacity A.
**(2) Calculating power demand to satisfy QOL requirements**
Power demand is broken down by summer / winter / mid-season, and an equipment operation schedule is created for the QOL equipment and other devices. Devices other than QOL equipment are set with reference to prior research (Fujimoto et al.). Since Fujimoto et al.'s study targets a highly-illuminated commercial facility, its device-usage time is restricted to nighttime lighting. This study, aiming for 24-hour biomass CHP operation, also examines device usage during daytime, and updates the schedule accordingly.

Shelter facility operations are assumed to require lighting as well (Hie, 2021), so a benchmark for lighting demand in disasters is also set. QOL Level 2 equipment (Table 1) plus other equipment are set as shown, with the necessary devices, lighting, and equipment quantities combined, and time-of-day power demand is calculated. QOL requirement "e" (thermal environment) uses a self-made calculation program, entering region No. and building floor area, and the extended-floor-area-parameterized cooling/heating load is calculated.

**(3) Comparing electricity supply and demand**
Based on the power supply set in (2), the electricity/heat supply from biomass CHP is compared with QOL-equipment demand to confirm whether demand can be met.
**(4) Calculating the number of people who can use bathing facilities: B**
Based on the heat supply determined in (3), the number of people able to use the bathing facility, matched to operation, is calculated from Equation ① below.
q = 50 L (per person, based on 2014 reference), tc = 16 °C (based on reference 7)
B [people/h] = H / (0.00116·q·(60−tc))  … Equation ①
H: biomass CHP heat output [kW/h]
q: hot-water use per person [L], tc: groundwater temperature [°C]

### 1.4 Cases examined
#### 1.4.1 Disaster-response retrofit of an existing biomass CHP facility
As an existing biomass CHP facility, Takayama City was selected, which needs a disaster response system (iii) providing heat to a bathing facility and (iv) using groundwater, and which has Japan's highest peak wood-biomass power output. A pellet-fired biomass power plant (hereafter "Takayama") with electrical output 165 kW and thermal output 260 kW was selected. Electricity is sold at ¥40/kWh under FIT; heat is used to warm the water at an adjacent bathing facility and for hot-water/floor heating, with heat demand roughly matching the daytime hot-spring bath refill demand; heat is used more efficiently by day than night, and a storage tank compensates. Fuel pellets are stockpiled on-site for 6 days, sourced from an adjacent pellet mill.

#### 1.4.2 New biomass CHP installation for a disaster-response system
Next, a disaster-response system is examined for the case of installing a new biomass CHP. First, a candidate area lacking a pellet mill and with adequate feedstock is chosen; Kumano City in Mie Prefecture, population 15,500, area 373.4 km², a "forest city" (Kumano City, 2021), with a forest cover rate of 88%, high aging rate of 44.6%, is selected, where revitalizing the region through use of woody biomass is expected. Kumano lacks a pellet mill, so wood chips, which are relatively easy to process, are used as fuel; the raw wood is sourced, as in the Takayama case, from high mountain areas, and the chip material is purchased from the Kumano-city timber market.

## 2. Results and Analysis
### 2.1 Verification of disaster-response retrofit of an existing biomass CHP facility
#### 2.1.1 Normal-time evaluation
Based on measured values, the created energy-flow diagram is shown in **Figure 2**. The total CO₂ reduction is 605 t-CO₂/year; since fuel production requires 1,030 t/year of raw log, this equals a per-tonne reduction of 0.587 t-CO₂/t-log. Compared with existing research (Yamazaki, 2022), the CO₂ reduction is larger, attributed to Takayama having more constant heat use than comparable cases.

**Figure 2: Energy-flow diagram (Takayama)**
- Mountain forest → 1,030 t/yr, 8,750 GJ/yr raw wood → material supplier → 1,030 t/yr, 7,590 GJ/yr → pellet plant
- Pellet plant → power grid: electricity 159,100 kWh/yr; pellets 620 t/yr, 11,000 GJ/yr → biomass CHP
- Biomass CHP: electricity 165 kW / 5,700 kWh, heat 260 kW; power 552,000 kWh/yr, heat 824,000 kWh/yr (net) → bathing facility (heat sold/used)
- CO₂ reduction: pellet plant power −695 t-CO₂/月 (month), biomass CHP power/heat −5,600 t-CO₂/月, total 605 t-CO₂/yr

Next, the resource/fund flow diagram is shown in **Figure 3**, using 2021 fiscal-year measured values and interview results, based on reference source (9). The resulting internal multipliers are LM3 = 2.20, LM4 = 2.40, which — compared with Yamazaki (2021)'s Kurayoshi City (LM3=2.02, LM4=2.28) and Hirata Town (LM3=2.00, LM4=2.31) — are equal or higher, indicating money is circulating adequately within the region. Since pellet fuel is used, fund circulation via the pellet plant is considered large, but the plant is limited to sites within the region. Considering versatility, adopting a biomass CHP that uses easily-obtainable chip fuel nationwide, and using on-site chip drying heat, is expected to yield further CO₂ reductions.

#### 2.1.2 Disaster-time evaluation
**(1) Calculation of shelter capacity: A**
The designated shelter space is 333 m², giving shelter capacity A = 95 people.
**(2) Calculation of power demand to satisfy QOL requirements**
For time-of-day power demand of Level-2 QOL equipment "e," the program uses region No. 6, building type "lodging facility," floor area 1,791.7 m². Cooling/heating COP values used were 3.10/3.51. Time-of-day power demand for summer, winter, and mid-season is calculated; the largest is summer, shown in **Figure 4**, with air conditioning accounting for most of the demand.

**Figure 4: Disaster-time hourly power-demand transition (Takayama, summer) — Fig. 4**
Bar chart, hourly power demand [kWh] on Y-axis (0–70), hours 9:00–19:30 on X-axis; stacked series legend: air conditioning (e), purification tank (acd), water pump (acd), groundwater pump (acd), washing machine (d), QOL equipment (b), lighting (a), required equipment. Air conditioning dominates the stack.

**(3) Comparison of electricity supply and demand**
Comparing the power demand from (2) with supply from biomass CHP, since the equipment has output-control characteristics and no scheduling flexibility, it can satisfy every QOL requirement across all seasons throughout the 6-day period. However, since a constant power supply/demand simultaneity is required at night when power output is curtailed, an output equal to the rated 165 kW plus a load-following storage battery matched to that output are needed, indicating high initial cost.

**Figure 5: Hourly power transition over the 6-day disaster period (Takayama)**
Line chart, power [kW] on Y-axis (0–180) over 6 days; three lines: summer demand, winter demand, mid-season demand — showing a repeating daily cyclical pattern each day.

**(4) Calculation of the number of people who can use bathing facilities: B**
Given that in this CHP, heat and constant output for other uses causes about 90% of heat loss during mid-distribution, and Equation ① is used, the number of people who can use the bathing facility is calculated as 825 people/day — more than 8 times shelter capacity A.

### 2.2 Verification of a new biomass CHP installation with disaster response
#### 2.2.1 System overview
Since Kumano City lacks a pellet mill, chips — relatively easy to process — are used as fuel. Raw wood is sourced from high mountains as in the Takayama case, and chip material is purchased from the Kumano-city timber market. Output-controllable equipment is adopted, so that a design accommodating both normal-time and disaster-time operation can reduce fuel consumption and enable long-duration continuous operation, is examined.

#### 2.2.2 Selecting facilities for installation
Considering the requirements for a disaster response system, a candidate facility with bathing capability is selected. Also, considering fuel-procurement ease and disaster-specific tsunami-flood safety, a facility at elevation 100 m or higher is chosen. Under these conditions, lodging facility H is selected as the target facility. Its floor area is 3,669 m², larger than Shibushi Town's 1,505 m², and its evacuee capacity is deemed sufficient. It is also equipped with a large bath and showers, so heat demand in normal and disaster times is required.

#### 2.2.3 Determining installed equipment and unit count
Since lodging facility H's heat demand is electricity-based, and normal-time hot-water supply/heating self-consumption is also modeled by time of day using program area No. 6, building type "lodging facility," floor area 3,669 m². Peak hot-water heating load in February was 148 kW, and annual hot-water heating load was calculated as 449 MWh. Since disaster-time operation must also match demand, and full off-grid operation must be enabled, a small gas-engine generator with 40 kW electrical / 100 kW thermal output was selected.

Since fuel drying is required to bring untreated raw material (chips) down to a target moisture content before feeding the biomass CHP, the necessary drying heat is calculated from Equation ②:
H = Hdry + Hwater − Hwet … Equation ②
Hdry = 17.3 [MJ/kg] × Wdry [kg]
Hwater = 2.45 [GJ/t] × (Wwet − Wdry) [t]
Hwet = 8.5 [MJ/kg] × Wwet [kg]
H: annual chip-drying required heat, Hdry: heat generated by dry chip, Hwater: heat of vaporization generated during drying, Wdry: dry chip consumption, Wwet: raw (wet) chip consumption

Using Equation ② and chip consumption, the necessary drying heat was found; comparing this with the previously calculated annual hot-water heating load (bathing facility heat demand) and the supply available from biomass CHP is shown in **Figure 6**. Biomass CHP is assumed to run 330 days per year.

#### 2.2.4 Normal-time evaluation
Energy-flow diagrams for the 2-unit biomass CHP installation are shown in Figure 8; CO₂ reduction results for the two cases are shown in Figure 9. Using storage tanks etc. to shift heat from biomass CHP to be used during the day rather than at night, CO₂ reduction with 1 unit is 0.647 t-CO₂/t-log, with 2 units it is 0.597 t-CO₂/t-log — the single-unit case shows larger reduction, because fully utilizing the CHP's heat in the single-unit case has greater impact. The dedicated on-site chip drying, versus Takayama's use of externally-supplied pellets, results in a smaller CO₂ reduction for the 2-unit case.

Next, LM3/LM4 are calculated for the two cases. Assuming FIT sell price ¥40/kWh, heat sale price based on light-oil equivalent ¥97/L (reference 19), the fund-flow diagram for the 2-unit installation is shown in **Figure 10**. From this, LM was calculated: 1 unit gives LM3=2.16, LM4=2.41; 2 units gives LM3=2.10, LM4=2.38. Heat utilization rate is high, so CHP-1 values are somewhat higher than CHP-2, but both compare favorably (equal or higher) with Yamazaki (2021)'s Kurayoshi and Hirata cases, confirming that funds circulate adequately within the region.

## 3. Overall Discussion
For the existing biomass CHP retrofit examined at Takayama, and the new biomass CHP installation examined at Kumano, results are compared in **Table 2**. In Kumano, going from 1 to 2 units reduced both CO₂ reduction and LM, because the single-unit case fully utilized all heat from the CHP, achieving a thorough system design. This demonstrates the importance of efficient-heat-use system design in normal-time operation. Meanwhile, in disaster-time operation, air conditioning dominates power demand overall, but the Takayama-case CHP (40 kW-equivalent to floor area) could cover about 20% of that demand (the parameter is the cooling/heating-load floor area). Although Takayama's shelter space (333 m²) is smaller than Kumano's, the 165 kW-rated CHP at Takayama has surplus power capacity but needed a large battery combined with it because heat is used to provide bathing-facility user capacity beyond shelter capacity, whereas at Kumano's 45 kW-class CHP (2 units), even though thermal output was smaller, bathing-facility user capacity still covered shelter capacity fully, and all QOL requirements were met while satisfying power demand, with output-controlled operation minimizing surplus power only at grid switch-over, achieving a nationally versatile "disaster response system" using chips.

## Conclusion
Aiming to make maximum use of biomass CHP performance during a disaster, this study proposed "disaster response systems," examining both a retrofit to an existing case and a new installation. Contributing to regional environmental/economic circular-ecological-sphere formation in normal times, and prospects were established for a "disaster response system" using low-fuel-consumption, off-grid-capable, small-scale wood-biomass CHP heat/power supply, achievable at low cost.

Going forward, disaster-response system design should be examined together with new evacuation-shelter design.
