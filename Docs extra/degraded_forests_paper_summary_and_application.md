# Paper Summary & Project Application

> **"The forest our ancestors planted — powering the village they built."**
> 先人が植えた森が、今、村に豊かさをもたらす。

## "Promoting Local Revitalization to Solve Issues on Degraded Forests in Japan"
*Ooba, Nakamura & Togawa — National Institute for Environmental Studies, Fukushima Branch (2020)*

---

## 1. Paper Summary

### What the Paper Is About

This academic study examines how a small depopulated mountain town in Fukushima Prefecture — **Mishima Town, Oku-Aizu** — addressed two linked crises: degraded, unmanaged forests and reliance on external fossil energy. The researchers (NIES) partnered with the town to design and pilot a **small-scale woody biomass combined heat and power (CHP) system** driven entirely by local forest resources.

### The Core Problem

Japanese rural forests have been degrading for decades due to:
- Depopulation and aging (Mishima: population <1,600; 53% elderly)
- Low domestic timber prices since the 1970s — imports undercut local wood
- Only ~45% of plantation forests actively managed
- Unclear and fragmented forest ownership boundaries
- Lack of workers for thinning and harvesting

After Japan's 2011 nuclear disaster, the Feed-in Tariff (FIT) system renewed interest in woody biomass power — but large-scale plants (requiring tens of thousands of tons/year) are not viable for small rural communities. The paper argues for a different, community-scaled approach.

### Two Key Constraints Identified

The researchers derived two critical planning constraints that apply to any small rural biomass project:

| Constraint | Details |
|---|---|
| **[1] Circulation radius** | Raw biomass transport: max 50–125 km; heat via pipes: only a few hundred meters; local power lines: a few km |
| **[2] Feasible facility size** | Mishima's potential supply was ~4,000 tons/year — enough for a small CHP (≤50 kW), not a large plant |

### Four Research Activities and Findings

| Research Activity | Key Finding |
|---|---|
| Forest resource survey (drone + LiDAR) | Clarified ownership boundaries and timber volumes; revealed most forests unknown/unmapped |
| Biomass production potential | Chip production viable at ≤¥7,000/m³ with intensive thinning; ~10,000 m³/year potentially harvestable |
| Energy demand survey (HEMS installed) | Small CHP consuming **~700–800 tons/year** of biomass is feasible for community + hot spring facilities |
| Social system and governance | A community council dividing supply and consumption tasks was the recommended structure |

### Social Implementation Steps Taken

- **Tree Station programme** — residents bring firewood logs, receive ¥4,500/m³ in local gift certificates
- **Wood-fired boiler** installed at Mishima Local Crafts Museum using locally collected firewood
- **Oku Aizu Five Towns Council** established under Ministry of Environment subsidy
- Regular joint meetings between NIES researchers and town officials, at least monthly

### Conclusion

The project aligns with Japan's **Regional Circular and Ecological Spheres (CES)** framework and the SDGs. A small-scale biomass CHP, even at community level, can:
- Revive forest management and increase carbon absorption
- Reduce external energy dependence
- Create stable local employment
- Reduce landslide/flood risk through proper forest management
- Act as the seed of a wider rural revitalization model (comparable to Germany's Stadtwerke system)

## 2. What Is Important for the Mitsue Project

Mitsue Village and Mishima Town are **strikingly parallel** in their conditions:

| Factor | Mishima Town | Mitsue Village |
|---|---|---|
| Forest cover | 88% | ~88% |
| Population trend | Depopulation, aging | Depopulation, aging |
| Forest type | Sugi (cedar) monoculture | Sugi (cedar) monoculture |
| Forest management | ~45% managed | Low management rate |
| Energy dependency | External fossil fuels | External fossil fuels |
| Location | Rural mountain, Fukushima | Rural mountain, Nara |
| Ownership clarity | Fragmented, unclear | Fragmented, unclear |

This makes the Mishima study one of the **most directly applicable precedents** available for the Mitsue project.

### 2A. Critical Data Points for Mitsue's Feasibility Studies

These numbers from the paper can be used as **planning benchmarks** for Mitsue's Phase 1 Feasibility Studies:

| Data Point | Value | How to Use |
|---|---|---|
| Biomass consumption for small CHP | **700–800 tons/year** | Baseline input for Mitsue's Energy Systems Feasibility Study |
| Chip production target price | **≤¥7,000/m³** | Cost threshold for Mitsue's Forestry Feasibility Study |
| Max harvestable volume (Mishima) | **~10,000 m³/year** | Scale reference; Mitsue feasibility study must determine its own figure |
| Recommended CHP size | **≤50 kW electricity output** | Sizing target for Phase 3 pilot CHP unit |
| Forest management rate (unmanaged Japan) | **~45%** | Baseline to justify the forest intervention component |
| Raw biomass transport limit | **50–125 km** | Confirms local sourcing from Mitsue's own forests is valid |
| Heat pipe supply range | **few hundred meters** | Confirms Koryukan building must be close to CHP unit |
| FIT scenario biomass consumption | **~800 tons/year profitable** | Confirms Phase 3 pilot can be structured to use FIT revenue |

### 2B. Forest Ownership Problem — Directly Applicable

The Mishima questionnaire revealed that forest owners' top barriers were:
1. **Low timber prices** (56 responses)
2. **Lack of manpower**
3. **Undeveloped forest roads**
4. **Unclear boundaries** (80 responses combined with road needs)

Mitsue almost certainly shares these exact barriers. The **drone + LiDAR forest survey** used in Mishima is a practical tool Mitsue can replicate in Phase 1 or 2 to:
- Map actual forest boundaries
- Estimate timber volumes per owner
- Build trust with landowners by showing them their own data
- Lay the groundwork for landowner partnership contracts

### 2C. Governance Model — Proven Structure

The Mishima council structure (supply working group + consumption working group under a coordination council) is a **tested model** for the Mitsue project's stakeholder organization. It:
- Prevents confusion between forestry/supply decisions and energy/demand decisions
- Includes both local and external stakeholders
- Was established with Ministry of Environment subsidy — a funding route Mitsue can pursue

### 2D. FIT System Caution

The paper explicitly warns that FIT high purchase prices are **not guaranteed long-term**. After 2017, large solar was excluded; the trend is toward wholesale markets. For Mitsue:
- **Do not design the project's financial model to depend entirely on FIT**
- Use FIT revenue in early phases (Phase 3) to prove viability
- Build toward self-sufficient energy pricing for the data center and EV charging as primary revenue

### 2E. Social Legitimacy — Start Small and Visible

Mishima's first steps were deliberately small (firewood exchange, museum boiler). This built:
- Visible proof that the idea works
- Trust with residents skeptical of top-down projects
- A supply chain prototype before large investment

For Mitsue, this validates the **Phase 0 and Phase 1 "trust-first" approach** in the implementation plan. A small, visible early win (e.g., a wood-fired heater in a community building) can do more for local trust than any presentation.

## 3. How to Use This for the Project to Succeed

### Phase 0–1 Actions (Months 1–9)

| Action | How the Paper Supports It |
|---|---|
| Private conversations with village leadership | Paper shows Mishima signed a formal MOU with NIES — start informally, formalize later |
| Commission Forestry Feasibility Study | Use Mishima's ≤¥7,000/m³ chip cost and 10,000 m³/year harvest target as benchmarks to validate or refine |
| Commission Energy Systems Feasibility Study | Use 700–800 tons/year biomass and ≤50 kW CHP as starting sizing assumptions |
| Approach landowners | Use the questionnaire findings to anticipate objections: timber price, labor, roads, boundaries |
| Propose drone + LiDAR forest survey | Frame it as a gift to landowners — clarifying their own assets — not a government-style intrusion |

### Phase 1–2 Actions (Months 4–18)

| Action | How the Paper Supports It |
|---|---|
| Set up community council structure | Model on Mishima's supply/consumption working group structure; apply for Ministry of Environment subsidy |
| Apply for Fukushima-style smart community subsidies | Nara Prefecture equivalents exist; Mishima used these for pre-feasibility studies |
| Publish bilingual forest/energy vision | Paper demonstrates academic credibility helps unlock institutional partnerships (NIES + university collaborations) |
| Design landowner revenue sharing | Mishima's gift certificate model (¥4,500/m³) is a prototype — Mitsue can develop cash or service-equivalent returns |

### Phase 3 Pilot (Months 19–30)

| Action | How the Paper Supports It |
|---|---|
| Install small CHP ≤50 kW | Directly validated by Mishima's feasibility findings |
| Source biomass locally within circulation radius | Raw biomass transport limit of 50–125 km confirmed; Mitsue's own forests are within this |
| Install HEMS in pilot buildings | Mishima used this to map real energy demand before finalizing system design — do this before finalizing data center load specs |
| Use FIT registration for electricity | Mishima's profitable scenario relied on FIT — register early before eligibility rules tighten further |

### Long-Term (Phase 4+)

| Action | How the Paper Supports It |
|---|---|
| Target Regional CES certification | Japan's Fifth Basic Environmental Plan explicitly supports regional CES projects — Mitsue fits the definition perfectly |
| Link to SDG reporting | Paper shows biomass + forest management directly satisfies SDGs 7, 13, 15 — useful for international investors and grant funders |
| Scale CHP only after supply chain is proven | Paper's central warning: demand always exceeds local supply if uncontrolled; keep harvesting sustainable |
| Develop climate hazard maps | Mishima researchers noted forest management reduces flood/landslide risk — Mitsue can use this for disaster-resilience branding |

## 4. Summary — Most Important Takeaways

1. **700–800 tons/year biomass for ≤50 kW CHP** is the right pilot size for Mitsue — validated by real data from a comparable village.
2. **Drone + LiDAR forest survey first** — you cannot plan supply without knowing what you actually have.
3. **Circulation radius is real** — design the system so biomass stays local and heat consumers are within a few hundred meters of the CHP.
4. **FIT is a tool, not a strategy** — use it in Phase 3 but build the long-term model on data center, EV charging, and heat sales revenues.
5. **Start small and visible** — a wood-fired boiler in a community building costs almost nothing and builds trust that no presentation can buy.
6. **Govern with a council structure** — supply and consumption working groups under one coordination body, including local and external stakeholders.
7. **Mitsue is not alone** — Mishima's model is a direct precedent; cite it in funding applications and with village leadership to show this has been done successfully.
