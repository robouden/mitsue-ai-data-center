If by **carbon footprint** you mean **how much carbon a tree removes from the atmosphere and stores**, the answer is a little nuanced. It depends on age, growth rate, wood density, and how the forest is managed.

### Young trees (0–30 years)

**Sugi** (Cryptomeria japonica)

- Grows very quickly.

- Can absorb **more CO₂ per year** than many broadleaf species during its rapid growth phase.

- This is why Japan planted millions of sugi after World War II.

Typical annual carbon uptake:

- **10–30 kg CO₂/year** for young trees.

- In productive plantations, sometimes considerably more.

---

### Mature forests (50–150+ years)

Broadleaf forests (especially oak, beech, chestnut)

Examples:

- Quercus serrata

- Fagus crenata

- Castanea crenata

Advantages:

- Wood is denser.

- Trees often live much longer.

- Soil accumulates more organic matter.

- Leaf litter produces richer forest soils.

Although annual growth is slower, **the total ecosystem carbon storage (trees + roots + soil)** is often **equal to or greater than that of a sugi plantation** over the long term.

---

## Carbon stored per cubic meter

Approximate values:

| Tree         | Wood density   | Carbon stored      |
| ------------ | -------------- | ------------------ |
| Sugi         | ~380 kg/m³     | ~190 kg carbon     |
| Japanese oak | ~650–750 kg/m³ | ~330–375 kg carbon |
| Beech        | ~600–700 kg/m³ | ~300–350 kg carbon |

Broadleaf hardwoods can therefore store **about 1.5–2× as much carbon per unit volume of wood** because they are denser.

---

## Soil carbon

One of the biggest differences is below ground.

A mature broadleaf forest typically develops:

- thicker humus

- richer fungal networks

- more soil organic carbon

- greater biodiversity

Many ecologists estimate that **30–60% of the carbon in an old broadleaf forest is stored in the soil**, whereas intensively managed conifer plantations often store a smaller fraction in the soil.

---

## Long-term comparison

Imagine one hectare over 100 years:

**Sugi plantation**

- Rapid early carbon uptake.

- Peaks relatively early.

- Often harvested every 40–60 years.

- Carbon remains stored only if the wood is used in long-lived products (e.g., buildings); otherwise, much returns to the atmosphere through decomposition or burning.

**Natural broadleaf forest**

- Slower accumulation.

- Continues storing carbon for centuries.

- Higher soil carbon.

- Greater resilience to pests, storms, and drought.

---

## Which is better?

It depends on the goal.

**For rapid CO₂ removal over the first few decades:**

- ✅ Sugi often performs better.

**For total long-term carbon storage and ecosystem health:**

- ✅ Mature broadleaf forests generally have the advantage.

**For climate resilience:**

- ✅ Mixed forests (sugi plus broadleaf species) are increasingly favored in Japan because they combine relatively fast growth with greater biodiversity, healthier soils, and improved resistance to disease and extreme weather.

Since you've been researching forest management around Mitsue Village, this is especially relevant. Much of the postwar sugi plantation in that region is now reaching maturity. Converting portions of those forests into **mixed sugi–broadleaf stands** can maintain timber production while increasing biodiversity, improving water retention, reducing landslide risk, and enhancing long-term carbon storage.
